# Domain & Credibility Checks (Conditional Layer)

Canonical, reusable credibility/fidelity checks for any syndication / deal / investor writing — content posts, investor updates, sales emails, deal promos.

**When to apply:** only when the draft makes claims about deals, returns, LP/GP mechanics, tax treatment, or investor specifics. A personal email, scheduling note, or generic message skips this layer.

---

## Domain Logic Validation (Deep Check)
Pressure-test EVERY factual claim: "Would an experienced GP or LP actually say this? Is this how the industry actually works?"
- Watch for stock/public-market mental models bleeding into real estate syndication context (e.g., "unrealized gains boosting net worth on paper" is a stock investing frame, not syndication)
- No blanket statements — if something is sometimes true but not always, hedge it ("often", "can", "in many deals") rather than stating it as universal truth
- Verify described industry practices match reality. Don't claim sponsors/operators do X if they typically don't. Don't claim investors think Y if that's not how this audience actually thinks.
- Every claim that references a specific practice, number, or industry norm must be grounded in the source material or flagged as needing verification

## Source Fidelity (Critical — prevents hallucination)
For EVERY personal claim, story, or first-person experience in the draft:
1. Identify the exact source (specific interview transcript, newsletter issue, podcast episode, call notes)
2. Verify the claim appears in that single source as stated
3. If a narrative was assembled by combining details from multiple sources into one story, **this is fabrication** — flag it and rewrite using only what a single source supports
4. If a personal claim cannot be traced to any source, **it is hallucinated** — remove it entirely

Additional checks:
- Are any statements presented as facts that are actually oversimplifications or false?
- Does the draft stay within what the source material actually says, or does it extrapolate beyond it?

## Factual Claim Verification (Critical — prevents confident falsehoods)
Every specific factual assertion — whether in an anecdote, a hypothetical scenario, or a declarative statement — must pass this gate:

1. **Identify all concrete claims.** A concrete claim is any statement about how an institution, process, product, regulation, or market mechanism works. "Fidelity would tell you no" is a concrete claim. "Most people don't know" is an opinion (fine). "You can roll a 401(k) into an SDIRA" is a concrete claim.
2. **For each concrete claim, verify the source:**
   - *Sourced from material:* The claim appears in or is directly supported by the provided source material. ✓
   - *Domain common knowledge:* The claim is widely known and verifiable within the target domain (e.g., "401(k) contributions are tax-deferred" for a financial audience). ✓
   - *Neither:* The claim cannot be traced to source material AND is not verifiable common knowledge. Flag it. Either (a) find a source, (b) hedge it ("in many cases," "typically"), or (c) cut it.
3. **Hypothetical scenarios get the same treatment.** A hypothetical framed as "if you did X, Y would happen" must be true — Y must actually be what would happen. The scenario is imagined; the underlying fact is not. If you're not confident Y is accurate, don't write the hypothetical.
4. **Absolute claims need absolute confidence.** Statements without hedging ("Fidelity would tell you no" vs "Fidelity would probably tell you no") require high confidence. If there are exceptions, edge cases, or nuances, either hedge the claim or add the qualifier. A confidently wrong specific claim is worse than a vague correct one — it damages credibility with knowledgeable readers and exposes the author to being publicly corrected.

## Internal Consistency (Numbers, References & Self-Contradiction)
- If you say "one question" or "one thing," verify you only listed one. If you listed two, say "two."
- If a transitional phrase references a setup (e.g., "The fix takes one conversation"), verify the setup actually exists and connects logically
- Numbers, counts, and quantified references must match the actual content written
- Every section transition should logically follow from what preceded it
- **Self-contradiction check:** Flag any absolute statement that is contradicted by surrounding sentences. Example: "The investors who chased tax benefits got obliterated" immediately followed by evidence that not all of them did. Either hedge the absolute ("many investors") or remove the contradicting evidence.
- **Sensitivity & empathy:** When discussing investor losses, capital destruction, or financial pain, maintain appropriate gravity. Real people lost significant money — sometimes life savings. Never minimize or make light of actual financial harm. Avoid phrases that could read as dismissive (e.g., "the psychological damage was worse" when some investors lost their net worth).

## Syndication Domain Knowledge (Reference)
These are common knowledge gaps that cause credibility failures. Verify every draft against these facts:
- **Active vs. passive income:** Syndication losses are passive losses. They offset passive income, NOT active/W-2 income (with limited exceptions like Real Estate Professional Status). Never imply syndication tax benefits will reduce someone's paycheck tax burden.
- **What LPs control:** Limited partners control TWO things: (1) due diligence — deciding whether to invest, and (2) allocation — how much capital to commit. They do NOT control operations, management decisions, or deal execution. Don't oversimplify to just one of these.
- **IRR is not one thing:** High IRR can mean many different things depending on hold period, cash flow timing, and return of capital. Never present IRR as a single metric with a single interpretation.
- **Sponsors don't show cash flow vs. appreciation split:** Most offering documents project total returns. The breakdown between cash flow and appreciation is rarely presented as a clean split for investors to evaluate side by side.
- **Capital loss sensitivity:** Some investors in the last cycle lost life savings to Ponzi schemes and fraudulent operators. Always treat capital loss with appropriate gravity and specificity — never as a generic cautionary tale.
- **LP returns are already net of the split:** The return figures a sponsor advertises to investors are after the GP's split and fees, not before. The waterfall/split does NOT whittle down the advertised LP return — that number is what's left once the split is taken. Never imply fees or the GP split "eat into," "reduce," or leave less of the returns an investor sees actually "reaching" them — that's a retail/stock-market mental model, and it inverts the exact misconception knowledgeable LPs push back on.
